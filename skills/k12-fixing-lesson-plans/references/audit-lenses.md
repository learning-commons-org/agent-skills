# Audit lenses, severity, and fix discipline

## The lenses

Run all of them; report only what the lesson actually shows. Three to six findings is the size a
teacher reads in a hallway — a list of twelve buries the one that matters.

**1. Alignment — does the lesson teach the whole or part of a standard it claims?**
Compare the standard's verb and object against what students are asked to produce. A lesson that
has students *identify* text evidence while the standard says *analyze how* the evidence supports
an argument is teaching a prerequisite and labeling it the standard. The reverse also happens: a
task demanding something the cited standard doesn't license, or a claimed skill no standard in the
lesson covers.

**2. Rigor ceiling — what is the highest-demand thing any student is asked to do?**
Judge the lesson's most demanding task against the grade-level demand from the progression or
learning components, not against how busy the lesson looks. Scaffolded and "differentiated"
versions get the same check: a support that converts reasoning into fill-in-the-blank has lowered
the ceiling for the students who most need to reach it.

**3. Instructional arc — do opening, middle, and closing form one path to the objective?**
A lesson can hit its target in the main activity and lose the thread by closing. Trace it as one
sequence: does the opening set up the thinking the main task requires, does each task depend on
the previous one, and does the closing ask about the objective rather than about the warm-up or
about how students felt? Name the specific place the thread breaks.

**4. Evidence of learning — could the teacher tell who learned it?**
For each check, ask what a correct response would prove. Tasks that let a student repeat a modeled
explanation back produce the same artifact from a student who reasoned and a student who copied,
so the check doesn't run. Note also what the teacher is meant to *do* with what they see.

**5. Range of proficiency — checked from both ends.**
Name the entry point for a student who arrives below the target, the scaffold that keeps the
cognitive work with the student rather than doing it for them, *and* the extension for a student
who already meets the target when they walk in. An audit that names a support and stops has
checked one end and left the other class of student invisible.

**6. Misconceptions — does the lesson anticipate how students actually go wrong?**
With documented error patterns for the skill in hand, check whether any task or check would
surface them. A lesson where the predictable wrong answer still earns full credit will send that
misconception forward intact.

**7. Practicality — timing, materials, clarity of directions, format.**
Real findings, and the cheapest to fix, which is exactly why they rank below the four above.

## Severity

Every finding carries one tag, because an untagged list asks the teacher to re-triage work the
audit already did:

- **[Blocks the objective]** — students can complete the lesson as written without doing what the
  standard requires, or the teacher will have no evidence of who did.
- **[Weakens learning]** — the target is reachable, but a predictable group of students won't reach
  it, or the evidence will be ambiguous.
- **[Polish]** — costs time or clarity; no learning is at stake.

## Drafting fixes that survive their own audit

- **Write at the grain of the lesson.** The replacement question text, the changed prompt, the
  revised timing — something the teacher pastes in. "Add more student discourse" costs the teacher
  time and returns advice they already had.
- **Ship the material the fix names.** A fix that rests on something outside the plan — a
  primary-source paragraph, a passage, a data set, a problem set, a set of numbers — carries that
  material in the audit at the length students will actually use, quoted from the real source. A
  named source with no text behind it hands the teacher the search instead of the fix; where the
  actual wording isn't reachable, the fix builds on material already in the lesson's own packet
  and says which. Include the sample student response in the language a student in that grade band
  would produce — an adult version of the target answer hides whether the task is doable.
- **Say what the student who already has it does.** Every fix that changes a task answers both
  ends: how a student below the target gets in, and what the student who finishes it correctly in
  four minutes does next. More of the same work is a holding pattern; the extension raises the
  demand inside the same objective — an added constraint, a counter-case, a claim to defend, a
  second representation to reconcile.
- **Resolve the structural question the fix opens.** A fix that adds or changes a task says whether
  it is core or optional, and what it displaces in the period. Building real material on top of an
  optional activity hands the teacher back the decision they came here to have made.
- **Attach the formative check to the new task.** Name what the teacher looks at and what separates
  a student who reasoned from one who repeated the model back — a revised diagram versus an
  unchanged one, a claim that names a different variable than the exemplar did.
- **Check the numbers before they reach the teacher.** Teachers paste model answers straight into
  the lesson, so a wrong one teaches the error with the audit's authority behind it. Solve every
  task you write, and plug the model answer back into the task's own stated conditions, not into
  the equation you set up for it. The equation can describe a different quantity than the prompt
  names, such as total area when the prompt asks about part of it. Any count or duration a finding
  states (minutes, problems, items) shows where it comes from, either the plan's labeled timing or
  the arithmetic itself ("8 + 14 = 22 of its 30 minutes"), so the teacher can check it against her
  copy.
- **Re-read the fix list against the findings list before writing the file.** A fix that
  reintroduces a gap you just flagged — reading load added back into a lesson flagged for access,
  a ceiling lowered inside an extension, a new task with no way to see student thinking — is
  caught here rather than by the teacher in period three.
